Sport Spectator Identification Scale (SSIS)

Overview

The Sport Spectator Identification Scale (SSIS) is a 7-item self-report measure of the degree to which an individual identifies with a sports team. Developed by Daniel Wann and Nyla Branscombe (1993), it is the most widely used measure of team identification in the sport management literature.

Important caveat: Lock & Heere (2017) argue the SSIS conflates two distinct theoretical constructs — see Limitations & Caveats below.

The Scale

Each item scored 1-8 (low to high). Items:

  1. How important to YOU is it that the [team] wins?
  2. How strongly do YOU see YOURSELF as a fan of the [team]?
  3. How strongly do your FRIENDS see YOU as a fan of the [team]?
  4. During the season, how closely do you follow the [team] via ANY of the following: in person, TV, radio, newspaper, internet?
  5. How important is being a fan of the [team] to YOU?
  6. How much do YOU dislike the [team's] greatest rivals?
  7. How often do YOU display the [team's] name or insignia at your place of work, where you live, or on your clothing?

Scoring: Sum or average across items. Higher scores = higher identification.

Psychometric Properties

From Wann & Branscombe (1993):

  • Internal consistency: Cronbach's α = .91
  • Test-retest reliability (1 year): r = .60, p < .001
  • Factor structure: Single factor, 66.9% variance explained
  • No gender differences in scores

Strengths

  • Brief (7 items) and easy to administer
  • Strong reliability and internal consistency
  • Extensively validated across many studies
  • Established as field standard, enabling cross-study comparison

Limitations & Caveats

The Lock & Heere (2017) Critique

This is the most significant methodological critique of the SSIS and should inform interpretation of all findings using this scale.

Lock & Heere (2017) demonstrate that the SSIS conflates two theoretically distinct constructs:

  1. Identity Theory (IT) — Stryker's framework about roles. "Being a fan" is a role one occupies, made salient through social commitment (network ties to role-related others).

  2. Social Identity Theory (SIT) — Tajfel & Turner's framework about group membership. Identifying with a team means incorporating it into one's social self, providing collective self-esteem.

How SSIS items mix these frameworks:

Item Theoretical Framework What It Actually Taps
Item 2 ("see yourself as a fan") Identity Theory Role self-perception
Item 5 ("being a fan important to you") Identity Theory Role importance
Item 3 ("friends see you as a fan") Identity Theory Social commitment to role
Item 1 ("important that team wins") Social Identity Theory Outcome-based group identification
Item 6 ("dislike rivals") Social Identity Theory Intergroup dynamics
Item 4 (following) Behavioral Identification manifestation, not identification itself
Item 7 (display) Behavioral Identification manifestation, not identification itself

Implications:

  • The scale has predictive validity (it correlates with outcomes) but questionable construct validity (we don't know what underlying construct it measures)
  • SSIS scores cannot be clearly interpreted as measuring either role-based or group-based identification
  • All findings based on SSIS are interpretively ambiguous regarding the theoretical mechanism
  • Items 4 and 7 conflate identification with its behavioral manifestations, creating potential circularity when using SSIS to predict behavior

Other Limitations

  • Sample base: Developed and primarily validated with US undergraduates, especially at universities with successful sports programs. Cross-cultural and non-college samples underrepresented.

  • Unidimensional assumption: Assumes identification is single construct, but may have distinct cognitive, affective, and evaluative components.

  • Measures state, not process: Assesses current identification level but cannot capture identification formation or change over time.

  • Context dependency: Original validation was with a highly successful team (KU basketball). Performance dynamics may affect scale behavior.

Alternative Measures

  • Psychological Commitment to Team (PCT): Mahony, Madrigal, & Howard (2000) — focuses on commitment/loyalty; does not resolve IT/SIT conflation
  • Team Identification Scale: Adapted versions by various authors
  • Points of Attachment Index: Trail, Robinson, Dick, & Gillentine (2003) — broader attachment objects
  • Team Identity Scale: Heere & James (2007) — attempts to address multidimensionality but developed before Lock & Heere critique

Note: No widely-adopted measure currently implements Lock & Heere's proposed distinction between fan identification (role-based) and team identification (group-based).

Usage Notes

When using SSIS in research or practice:

  • Recognise it conflates role identity and group identity constructs
  • Acknowledge predictive validity does not equal construct clarity
  • Be cautious interpreting what score changes mean theoretically
  • Sample characteristics matter for interpretation
  • Cannot be used to study identification formation (need longitudinal designs)
  • When reporting findings, note the Lock & Heere critique as a methodological caveat
