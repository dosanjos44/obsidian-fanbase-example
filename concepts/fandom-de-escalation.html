Fandom De-escalation

Definition

Fandom de-escalation refers to the process by which previously identified fans reduce and ultimately sever their psychological connection to a sports team. Unlike temporary fluctuations in engagement (e.g., reduced attention during off-season), de-escalation involves a fundamental identity shift from "fan" to "non-fan."

The concept originates from Hyatt & Foster (2015), building on earlier calls from Funk & James (2001) and Mullin et al. (2007) to understand "downward movement" on the psychological continuum and "dropouts" from the consumption escalator.

Theoretical Basis

Fandom de-escalation is grounded in identity work theory (Sveningsson & Alvesson, 2003; Lawrence & Suddaby, 2006), which posits that identities are not static possessions but ongoing accomplishments requiring continuous effort to maintain. Being a fan involves feeling, speaking, and acting in ways that identify the person as a fan. When this identity work is disrupted or abandoned, the fan identity dissolves.

Identity work theory distinguishes between:

  1. Cognitive/emotional dimensions: The values, beliefs, and feelings connecting fan to sport object
  2. Material/practical dimensions: The behaviours and practices that enact fandom (watching, discussing, purchasing, attending)

Both dimensions require continuous reinforcement for fandom to persist.

How It Works

Hyatt & Foster (2015) propose a three-stage model of fan de-escalation:

Stage 1: Maintaining Fandom

Fan identity is sustained through two parallel processes:

  • Cognitive & emotional escalation (1): Deepening the values alignment between fan and sport object
  • Material escalation (2): Routinising fan practices (watching, discussing, consuming)

When values are aligned (A) and practices are unimpeded (B), fans reinforce their values (3) and routinise their practices (4), maintaining stable identification.

Stage 2: Disrupting Fandom

Disruption occurs via two pathways (which can operate independently or simultaneously):

Pathway A — Value Misalignment (5):
When the sport object (team, league, sport) no longer represents what the fan believes it should represent, cognitive and emotional conflict (C) emerges. The fan feels discomfort from the gap between personal values and perceived sport object values.

Examples: Perceiving the league as greedy after a lockout; feeling the team prioritises profit over winning; becoming disillusioned with hockey's violence culture.

Most fans attempt to re-align fan values (7) by rationalising, justifying, or minimising the conflict. Fans who successfully repair continue fandom. Those who cannot repair move to de-escalation.

Pathway B — Practical Barriers (6):
When life circumstances prevent engagement in fan practices, practical impediments (D) emerge. The fan cannot enact the routines that embody fandom.

Examples: Relocation to a region without access to broadcasts; new relationship/job consuming time; having children; the sport object becoming unavailable (lockout).

Most fans attempt to remove barriers to fan practices (8) by adjusting schedules, finding new access methods, or prioritising differently. Those who cannot remove barriers move to de-escalation.

Stage 3: De-escalating

When repair fails, de-escalation occurs:

  • Cognitive and emotional de-escalation (9): The fan accepts that values cannot be re-aligned; the conflict becomes permanent. The fan stops cheering and embraces non-fan identity.

  • Material de-escalation (10): The fan accepts that practical impediments cannot be removed; routines shift away from fandom permanently.

Critical insight: Non-fandom is not passive absence but an actively maintained identity:

  • Enhancing cognitive and emotional conflict (11): Non-fans reinforce why the sport object's values conflict with theirs (e.g., mocking sports coverage, emphasising greed/violence)
  • Embedding practical impediments (12): Non-fans fill time with alternative activities that preclude fan practices (new hobbies, other sports, family activities)

The more actively maintained the non-fan identity, the harder re-engagement becomes.

Key Sources

  • sources/hyatt-foster-2015-deescalation-fandom — develops the identity work theory model; empirical study of 23 former NHL fans identifying seven exit themes (sport, hockey, league, team, players, media, life). League-level issues (19/23) dominated.

  • sources/martin-goldman-2015-detachment-process — develops a four-phase sequential process model (breakdown trigger → iterative decline → disengagement incident → exit) from customer-brand dissolution literature. Empirical study of 14 lapsed season ticket holders from a South African Super Rugby franchise. Complements Hyatt & Foster: where Hyatt & Foster explain why fans exit (value misalignment vs practical barriers), Martin & Goldman explain how exit unfolds temporally. The iterative decline phase is identified as the primary intervention window for "restorative actions."

  • sources/wann-1996-origination-continuation-cessation — earlier cessation research finding success, time/commitments, player loss, geography, and peer influence as top cessation factors. Hyatt & Foster extend this with deeper mechanism.

  • Funk & James (2001) — PCM paper that called for research on downward movement; both 2015 papers directly answer this call from different angles.

  • Mullin, Hardy & Sutton (2007) — sport marketing text noting "dropouts" from the frequency escalator; called for more research on adult dropouts.

Contested Aspects

  • Pathway independence: Hyatt & Foster's model treats value misalignment and practical barriers as potentially independent pathways, but empirically they often co-occur. The 2004-05 lockout created both value conflict (greed) AND practical barriers (nothing to watch). Whether pure single-pathway de-escalation occurs frequently is unclear.

  • Reversibility: The model implies de-escalation is difficult to reverse once non-fan identity is actively maintained, but does not test this empirically. Some former fans speculated children might pull them back; whether this actually occurs is unknown.

  • Generalisability: The study used NHL fans during a period of extreme disruption (full-season lockout). Whether de-escalation dynamics differ for sports without such dramatic events is untested.

  • Team vs. league vs. sport exit: Hyatt & Foster studied exit from team fandom specifically. Whether this differs from exit from sport fandom entirely is not distinguished — their participants who remained hockey fans (watching national teams) had exited team fandom but not sport fandom.

Relationship to Adjacent Concepts

  • concepts/fandom-as-career — Crawford (2003) provides the framework for understanding fandom as a bi-directional career trajectory; Hyatt & Foster provide the mechanism for downward movement. Together they offer a complete process account: Crawford tells us fandom has a career shape with possible regression; Hyatt & Foster explain how regression happens.

  • concepts/psychological-continuum-model — the PCM describes upward movement (Awareness → Attraction → Attachment → Allegiance); de-escalation is the reverse journey that Funk & James (2001) explicitly called for but did not theorise.

  • concepts/team-identification — high identification is often treated as stable; de-escalation demonstrates that even allegiant fans can fully exit. SSIS and similar measures capture current state, not trajectory or vulnerability.

  • concepts/birging-corfing — BIRGing/CORFing represent temporary, tactical adjustments to identification displays; de-escalation represents fundamental identity abandonment, not tactical distancing.

  • gaps/re-engagement-lapsed-fans — de-escalation theory directly informs re-engagement strategy: understanding why and how fans exited tells us what conditions must change for return.

Gaps

  • Re-engagement mechanisms: The model implies difficulty but does not test re-engagement. What actually triggers former fans to return? Does reverse socialisation (children → parent) work?

  • Vulnerability factors: What distinguishes fans who repair from fans who de-escalate when facing similar disruptions? Individual differences (age, attachment style, life stage) may matter but are unexplored.

  • Prevention strategies: What can teams/leagues do during Stage 2 (Disrupting) to facilitate repair rather than de-escalation? Early intervention possibilities are not addressed.

  • Non-traumatic exit: Hyatt & Foster's sample included many who exited via dramatic events (lockout, violence disgust, expansion). Gradual, mundane drift out of fandom (simple attrition) may follow different dynamics.

  • Cross-sport comparison: Does de-escalation work differently in sports with different cultures (e.g., football without fighting, cricket with multi-day tests)?

Applied Note

For fan development and re-engagement strategy, de-escalation theory provides three critical insights:

1. Prevention is far more efficient than recovery.
Once non-fan identity is established and actively maintained (enhancing conflict, embedding impediments), the switching costs back to fandom are high. The strategic priority is keeping fans in the "repair" phase of Stage 2 rather than letting them complete de-escalation. This requires: monitoring for signs of value misalignment (complaints, criticism), rapid response to barrier creation (access issues, life changes), and relationship maintenance during league-wide disruptions.

2. League-level causes dominate, but teams bear the consequences.
19/23 participants cited league issues as contributing to exit. Teams may have limited power to recover fans alienated by lockouts, rule changes, expansion, or sport-wide violence culture. This creates a collective action problem: leagues have less incentive than teams to prevent exit, but league behaviour drives most exit. Teams should advocate aggressively at league level for policies that protect fan relationships.

3. Practical-barrier exits are more recoverable than value-misalignment exits.
Fans who exited due to life circumstances (relocation, time, family) can potentially return if barriers are removed — the values alignment may still be latent. Fans who exited due to deep value conflicts (perceiving the sport object as greedy, violent, inauthentic) require the sport object itself to change. Re-engagement marketing cannot solve value misalignment; only substantive change can.

For Sporting Cultural Architecture: The de-escalation model suggests that the live experience and community infrastructure that produce "thick" attachment (Giulianotti's supporters) may create higher repair rates than "thin" consumer relationships (fans/flâneurs). When disruption occurs, supporters embedded in social networks and local community have more resources for repair; isolated consumers may simply switch to competing entertainment options.
