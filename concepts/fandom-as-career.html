Fandom as Career

Definition

A theoretical framework that conceptualises sport fandom as a career — a fluid, bi-directional trajectory through which individuals move across their life course — rather than as a fixed identity state or categorical type. Proposed by Garry Crawford (2003), drawing on the Chicago School sociological tradition (Hughes, Becker, Goffman, Glaser & Strauss).

The career concept emphasises that supporter identities are:

  • Processual: unfolding over time, not fixed at a moment
  • Socially constructed: shaped by interactions, relationships, and context
  • Bi-directional: capable of progression, regression, stagnation, or exit
  • Non-exclusive: individuals may hold multiple career positions across different sports/teams

Theoretical Basis

Rooted in Chicago School interactionist sociology, specifically:

  1. Everett Hughes — developed the concept of "career" as a sociological (not just occupational) category; careers involve subjective identity changes as well as objective position changes

  2. Howard Becker — applied career thinking to deviance and cultural participation; showed how people move through stages of involvement via social learning and network integration

  3. Erving Goffman — "moral career" concept; careers involve changes in self-conception and social identity, not just behaviour

  4. Glaser & Strauss — "status passage" theory; transitions between career stages are socially organised and involve negotiation

Also draws on:

  1. Michel Maffesoli — neo-tribes concept; contemporary social groupings are fluid, temporary, consumer-oriented, and overlapping rather than fixed and exclusive

The framework explicitly rejects:

  • Typological thinking (Giulianotti, Hunt et al.) — treating supporter identities as fixed categorical types
  • Incorporation/Resistance paradigm (Frankfurt School) — dividing fans into authentic resisters vs. passive dupes
  • Authenticity hierarchies — the notion that some fans are more "real" than others

How It Works

The Seven Career Stages

Crawford proposes a trajectory of increasing involvement:

│  7. APPARATUS                                                    │
│     Sport as occupation (journalists, employees, academics)      │
│     Structural relationship to sport differs fundamentally       │
├─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┤
│  6. PROFESSIONAL                                                 │
│     Expert knowledge; may contribute to supporter culture        │
│     Sport central to identity and social networks                │
├─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┤
│  5. DEVOTED                                                      │
│     Deep commitment; regular attendance; extensive knowledge     │
│     Primary social networks organised around sport               │
├─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┤
│  4. ENTHUSIASTIC                                                 │
│     Active participation; developing expertise                   │
│     Sport becoming important to identity                         │
├─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┤
│  3. ENGAGED                                                      │
│     Regular but not central; follows team/sport                  │
│     Attends occasionally; identifies as supporter                │
├─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┤
│  2. INTERESTED                                                   │
│     Casual awareness; occasional consumption                     │
│     No strong identification; entertainment-oriented             │
├─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┤
│  1. GENERAL PUBLIC                                               │
│     No active involvement; may be aware sport exists             │
│     Potential entry point for new supporters                     │
└─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘

Key Dynamics

Entry points vary: Unlike childhood origination models, the career framework allows entry at multiple points. Adults often enter at Interested or Engaged via:

  • Work colleagues
  • Romantic partners
  • Entertainment seeking
  • Community/neighbourhood participation

Movement is bi-directional: Supporters can:

  • Progress upward (intensification)
  • Regress downward (disengagement, lapsing)
  • Exit entirely (career termination)
  • Re-enter after lapsing (career resumption)
  • Stagnate at any level (career plateau)

Multiple careers possible: An individual may simultaneously hold different career positions with different sports or teams (e.g., Devoted for football, Interested for cricket).

Career stages are not types: The same person moves through multiple stages across their life course. Treating any stage as a fixed identity is a category error.

Contested Aspects

  • Empirical validation: The seven stages are analytically proposed, not empirically derived. No quantitative validation or operationalisation exists.

  • Tension with identity frameworks: The emphasis on fluidity may underweight the genuine fixedness of identity-based fandom documented in social identity research (Wann, James). Some fans do have stable, persistent identification that is not well-described as "career movement."

  • Neo-tribal framing critiqued: The Maffesoli-derived emphasis on fluid, temporary allegiances may overstate consumer orientation and understate the enduring, community-grounded relationships documented in traditional supporter cultures.

  • Context dependency: Crawford's case study (Manchester Storm) involved a new team in a non-traditional sport with primarily adult-acquired fans. The career model may fit this context better than established sports with multi-generational supporter bases.

  • Relationship to PCM unclear: Crawford does not engage with the Psychological Continuum Model (Funk & James 2001), which addresses similar stage-progression questions from a sport management tradition. The relationship between the frameworks is untheorised.

Relationship to Adjacent Concepts

  • concepts/psychological-continuum-model — The PCM describes psychological stages (Awareness → Attraction → Attachment → Allegiance); Crawford adds biographical temporality and explicit bi-directionality. Synthesis potential: PCM describes what happens psychologically at each stage; career model describes how people move through stages across the life course. The PCM's "Allegiance" roughly corresponds to Crawford's Devoted/Professional, but the career model explicitly theorises downward movement that the PCM mentions but doesn't develop.

  • concepts/spectator-taxonomy-giulianotti — Crawford directly critiques Giulianotti's typology for treating supporter identities as fixed categorical types. The career framework is positioned as an alternative that dissolves authenticity hierarchies.

  • concepts/fan-typologies — Crawford's critique applies to all typological frameworks: they snapshot a moment in an ongoing career and mistake position for identity.

  • concepts/childhood-origination — The career model encompasses childhood entry as one possible pathway (early entry, typically slow progression through stages) but does not privilege it.

  • concepts/adult-origination — Crawford's Manchester Storm data documents adult entry routes, providing empirical content for this pathway.

  • concepts/team-identification — High identification (SSIS) would correspond to higher career stages (Devoted, Professional), but the career model adds temporal dynamics that static identification measures cannot capture.

  • concepts/fandom-de-escalation — Hyatt & Foster (2015) provide the exit mechanism for the career framework. Crawford theorises that careers can terminate, but does not specify how or why exit occurs. Hyatt & Foster's identity work theory model explains the mechanism: value misalignment or practical barriers disrupt fan identity work, and when repair fails, non-fan identity is actively constructed and maintained. Together, Crawford and Hyatt & Foster offer a complete bi-directional account: Crawford describes career shape (including regression and exit possibilities); Hyatt & Foster explain the psychological process of exit.

  • concepts/fan-socialisation — Kolbe & James (2000) provide the entry mechanism for the career framework. Crawford documents that adults enter at various points via work colleagues, partners, and entertainment seeking, but does not specify the socialisation process by which entry occurs. Kolbe & James show that different socialising agents operate at different life stages (fathers for preteens, players/coaches for adults). Together, Kolbe & James and Hyatt & Foster form entry-exit bookends for the career framework: Kolbe & James explain how people enter the career; Hyatt & Foster explain how they exit.

Gaps

  • No operationalisation: The career stages lack measurement instruments or clear diagnostic criteria. How do you determine which stage someone is in?

  • Stage transition mechanisms: Crawford describes that movement happens but not how or why. What causes progression? What causes regression? What predicts career trajectory?

  • Relationship to childhood origination: If childhood-acquired fans have different career dynamics than adult-acquired fans, this is not developed. Do childhood fans move through stages differently? Are they more resistant to regression?

  • Re-engagement mechanisms: The model allows for career resumption after lapsing but does not specify what facilitates re-engagement. sources/hyatt-foster-2015-deescalation-fandom provides partial fill — identifies which former fans are more recoverable (practical-barrier exits vs. value-misalignment exits) and notes reverse socialisation (children pulling parents back) as a potential mechanism. Actual re-engagement remains empirically unstudied.

  • Cross-cultural validity: Single UK case study. Does the career model apply in different sporting cultures?

  • No PCM integration: The frameworks are complementary but have never been formally connected.

Applied Note

The career framework is the most important concept in this wiki for thinking about lapsed fans and re-engagement — a critical gap in both the literature and standard sports marketing practice.

Implications for Sporting Cultural Architecture

1. Lapsed fans are not lost — they are regressed careers

Standard CRM treats lapsed fans as "churned customers" to be re-acquired through new-customer tactics. The career model suggests they should be treated as individuals with career history who have regressed but retain:

  • Knowledge and expertise accumulated at higher stages
  • Social network ties (possibly attenuated) from previous engagement
  • Identity residue from previous career position

Re-engagement should acknowledge and activate this history, not treat lapsed fans as blank slates.

2. Diagnose career position, not fan "type"

Segmentation based on typologies (casual/avid, supporter/consumer) mistakes a moment for an identity. Segmentation should instead identify:

  • Current career position
  • Trajectory direction (progressing, regressing, stagnant)
  • Entry pathway (childhood vs. adult; which entry point)
  • Risk factors for regression

3. Design for stage transitions, not stage maintenance

Most fan engagement programmes focus on engaging existing fans at their current level. The career model suggests focus should be on:

  • Facilitating upward movement (especially the Enthusiastic → Devoted transition, which involves primary social network reorganisation)
  • Preventing downward movement (identifying regression risk factors)
  • Enabling re-entry after lapsing

4. Adult acquisition has multiple entry points

The Manchester Storm data shows adults enter through work colleagues, partners, entertainment seeking, and community participation. Sports properties should:

  • Map these entry pathways
  • Design touchpoints for each entry point
  • Recognise that adult entrants may progress rapidly or stagnate at Interested/Engaged

5. The Apparatus category matters

Journalists, academics, club employees have a structurally different relationship to the team. They are valuable for expertise and influence but are not representative of the supporter base. Marketing that relies heavily on Apparatus-stage voices may misread the broader supporter career landscape.

Connection to PCM

Use both frameworks together:

  • PCM diagnoses psychological state: Is this person at Attraction or Attachment? Are they intrinsically or extrinsically connected?
  • Career model diagnoses biographical trajectory: How did they get here? Are they progressing or regressing? What is their career history?

A fan at PCM's "Attraction" stage might be:

  • A childhood-origin fan who has regressed from Devoted to Engaged (career regression)
  • An adult-origin fan who recently entered at Interested and is progressing (career progression)

These require different interventions despite similar current psychological states.
