Fan Typologies

Definition

Fan typologies are classification schemes that distinguish different types of sports spectators based on underlying dimensions such as motivation, identification intensity, mode of relationship to teams/clubs, or behavioural expression. Two major typologies exist in the literature:

  1. Giulianotti (2002): Four types — supporters, followers, fans, flâneurs — based on traditional-consumer and hot-cool axes
  2. Hunt, Bristol & Bashaw (1999): Five types — temporary, local, devoted, fanatical, dysfunctional — based on situational-enduring motivation and centrality to self-concept

These typologies emerge from different disciplinary traditions and serve different purposes.

Theoretical Basis

Giulianotti draws on:

  • Figurational sociology (Elias, Taylor, Critcher)
  • Bryan Turner's hot-cool solidarity framework
  • Critical theory on commodification and the flâneur (Benjamin, Baudelaire)

Hunt et al. draw on:

  • BIRGing/CORFing (Cialdini et al. 1976)
  • Information processing and schema theory (Fiske & Taylor 1984)
  • Self-concept and attachment theory (Belk 1988, Ball & Tasaki 1992)

How It Works

                     |

  • Traditional-consumer axis: Is the relationship market-mediated or culturally/communally grounded?
  • Hot-cool axis: Is identification thick, permanent, and obligatory — or thin, transient, and voluntary?

Hunt et al.'s Five Types

Type Motivation Constraint Self-Centrality
Temporary Situational Time-bound Not central
Local Situational Geography-bound Peripheral
Devoted Enduring None Important
Fanatical Enduring None Very important
Dysfunctional Enduring None Primary/total

Key distinction: temporary and local fans have situational motivation (external triggers); devoted, fanatical, and dysfunctional fans have enduring motivation (internally anchored in self-concept).

Comparison: Two Traditions, Two Logics

Dimension Giulianotti (2002) Hunt et al. (1999)
Discipline Sport sociology Consumer research / marketing
Purpose Understand spectator identities under commodification Provide marketable segmentation
Key axes Traditional-consumer + hot-cool Situational-enduring motivation + self-centrality
Types 4 (supporter, follower, fan, flâneur) 5 (temporary, local, devoted, fanatical, dysfunctional)
Community/culture Central (topophilia, subcultural capital, class) Absent
Market forces Explicitly theorised (hypercommodification) Assumed as neutral backdrop
Empirical basis None (theoretical) None (conceptual)
Pathology Treats flâneur as parasitic but not deviant Pathologises "dysfunctional" fan as antisocial

Critical difference: Giulianotti asks what kind of relationship fans have (market-mediated vs traditional, thick vs thin). Hunt et al. ask how central fandom is to self and how constrained by situation. These are orthogonal questions. A Giulianotti "supporter" and a Giulianotti "fan" might both be "devoted" in Hunt's scheme — but their relationships to the club are structurally different.

Contested Aspects

  • Proliferation critique: Stewart, Smith & Nicholson (2003) argued that the proliferation of fan typologies in the late 1990s–early 2000s lacked empirical grounding and created conceptual confusion. Each new typology added categories without integrating prior work or testing whether the types actually exist in spectator populations.
  • Empirical vacuum: Neither Giulianotti nor Hunt et al. tested their typologies. Subsequent work has not systematically validated either.
  • Incommensurability: The two typologies are difficult to reconcile because they use different dimensions. A synthesis would require specifying how traditional-consumer relates to situational-enduring, and how hot-cool relates to self-centrality.
  • Cultural specificity: Giulianotti's types are shaped by UK/European football culture; Hunt et al.'s by US major league sports. Neither addresses cross-cultural applicability.
  • Career critique (Crawford 2003): Crawford argues that all typologies are fundamentally flawed because they treat supporter identities as fixed categorical types rather than fluid positions within an ongoing career. The "real fan" question implied by typological hierarchies is unanswerable and unproductive. The alternative: understand fandom as a career trajectory through which individuals move bi-directionally across the life course. See concepts/fandom-as-career.

Relationship to Adjacent Concepts

  • concepts/team-identification — the sport management tradition's continuous measure of identification intensity; does not distinguish types but provides a scale
  • concepts/birging-corfing — Hunt et al. use BIRGing to explain temporary fans specifically
  • concepts/hypercommodification — Giulianotti's structural driver of movement toward flâneur identity
  • concepts/psychological-continuum-model — complementary process model; typologies describe what kind of fan someone is at a given moment, while the PCM describes how they got there and how to move them. The frameworks are not competing — use typologies for diagnosis, PCM for intervention strategy
  • concepts/fandom-as-career — Crawford's alternative to typological thinking; treats supporter identities as positions within a fluid, bi-directional career rather than fixed types. The career model dissolves the authenticity hierarchies implicit in typologies and focuses on temporal trajectory rather than categorical classification

Gaps

  • No integrated framework combining insights from both traditions
  • No empirical validation of either typology's categories
  • No account of how fans move between types (either within a typology or across them) — though the concepts/psychological-continuum-model addresses this at the process level by describing stage transitions rather than type classifications
  • No typology addresses origination — how people become fans in the first place — only describes existing fans
  • Gendered dimensions unexamined in both

Applied Note

Sports marketers tend to reach for Hunt et al. because it is segmentation-ready: each type maps to tactics. But this accessibility comes at a cost. The typology obscures the structural and cultural dimensions that Giulianotti foregrounds.

For Sporting Cultural Architecture, the critical insight is what each typology reveals about the other's blind spots:

  • Hunt et al.'s blind spot: Two fans can be equally "devoted" (self-concept-central, performance-independent) but have radically different relationships to the club — one grounded in community and tradition, the other mediated entirely by market. Treating them the same misses what makes supporter culture valuable and vulnerable.

  • Giulianotti's blind spot: His typology does not segment by marketing value or tractability. Supporters may be the most authentic but least susceptible to activation campaigns; flâneurs are fickle but responsive to fashion.

A mature fan development strategy requires both lenses: understand the structural position of different spectator types (Giulianotti), then design targeted interventions (Hunt et al.) — without collapsing the distinction between them.
