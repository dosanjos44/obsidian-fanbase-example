Sports Fans: Measuring Degree of Identification with Their Team

Core Claim

The Sport Spectator Identification Scale (SSIS) is a valid and reliable 7-item measure of the degree to which individuals identify with a sports team. Persons who strongly identify exhibit distinct behavioral patterns (greater involvement and investment), cognitive patterns (self-serving attributions, positive expectations), and affective patterns (viewing team as special) compared to moderate or low identifiers.

Key Findings / Arguments

  • The SSIS has strong psychometric properties: internal consistency α = .91, test-retest reliability over one year r = .60, p < .001, single-factor structure explaining 66.9% of total variance (p. 4-5)
  • High identifiers attend significantly more home and away games, have been fans longer, and are more likely to attend future games than moderate or low identifiers (p. 5-6)
  • High identifiers are willing to invest more: pay more for tickets, wait longer in lines, pay more for championship/playoff games (p. 6)
  • High identifiers display self-serving attributional bias: attribute team success to internal factors (team ability), failure to external factors (bad luck, referees) — ego-protective pattern (p. 6, 9-10)
  • High identifiers have more positive expectations for future team performance, even controlling for past record (p. 8)
  • High identifiers view team and fellow fans as "special" and unique; friends are more likely to also be fans (p. 8, 11-12)
  • Even after defeats, highly identified persons maintain positive team outlook; low identifiers show ceiling effect around 7.0 on 1-8 scale (p. 9-10)
  • Identification predicts behavioral and affective responses more than other factors like team success (p. 10)

Methods

Two studies with University of Kansas undergraduates (introductory psychology, partial course credit):

  • Study 1: N = 188 (75 female, 113 male), mean age 18.5 years. Factor analysis and reliability assessment. Subjects categorized into high (M = 7.15), moderate (M = 5.68), and low (M = 3.55) identification groups.
  • Study 2: N = 303 (175 female, 128 male), mean age 18.9 years. Predictive validity testing with KU basketball team.

Sample limitations: Exclusively US undergraduates at a single university with a successful basketball program. College team context, not professional. Predominantly young sample. No cross-cultural or cross-sport validation.

Contributions to Fandom Theory

This is the foundational measurement paper for team identification in the sport management tradition. Before SSIS, no validated scale existed for assessing spectator identification. The paper:

  • Operationalizes team identification as a measurable individual difference variable
  • Demonstrates that identification level predicts behavioral outcomes (attendance, investment) and psychological processes (attributions, expectations)
  • Establishes the theoretical link between team identification and Social Identity Theory (Tajfel) / BIRGing research (Cialdini)
  • Creates the methodological foundation for decades of subsequent quantitative research on fan behavior

Limitations & Caveats

  • Sample: Exclusively US undergraduates at one university; generalisability to other populations, professional sports, non-US contexts untested

  • Context: KU basketball was highly successful at the time; scale validation with unsuccessful teams limited

  • Theoretical looseness — the Lock & Heere (2017) critique: The original SSIS was developed without rigorous theoretical grounding. Wann & Branscombe draw loosely on Social Identity Theory (Tajfel & Turner) and BIRGing research, but also implicitly draw on Identity Theory (Stryker) without recognising these are distinct frameworks. Lock & Heere (2017) later demonstrate this conflation in detail:

    • SIT concerns group-based identification (the team as part of one's social self)
    • IT concerns role-based identification ("being a fan" as a role one occupies)
    • SSIS items tap both frameworks inconsistently, mixing role identity questions (items 2, 5), social commitment measures (item 3), group identification markers (items 1, 6), and behavioral manifestations (items 4, 7)
    • This means the SSIS has predictive validity (it correlates with outcomes) but questionable construct validity (we don't know what it's actually measuring)
    • All subsequent research using the SSIS inherits this theoretical ambiguity

  • Conflation of identification with manifestations: Items 4 (following) and 7 (display) measure behaviors that are consequences of identification, not identification itself. This creates potential circularity when using SSIS to predict behavior.

  • Unidimensionality assumption: Treats identification as single factor, but the construct may have multiple dimensions (cognitive, affective, evaluative) that the scale collapses

  • Cross-sectional: Cannot establish causal direction — does high identification cause attendance, or vice versa?

  • No attention to identification formation: Measures current state, says nothing about how identification develops or changes over time

Applied Note

The SSIS provides a practical tool for segmenting fan populations by identification level. The finding that high identifiers behave differently (attend more, invest more, maintain loyalty through losses) has direct implications for fan development strategy. However, the scale measures current identification states, not pathways to identification. For Sporting Cultural Architecture, the key question the paper cannot answer is: how do you move someone from low to high identification? The scale can track whether interventions work, but doesn't reveal what interventions to try.

The Lock & Heere critique adds a further caution: even when using SSIS to track intervention effectiveness, we cannot clearly interpret what changed. Did group feelings strengthen? Did role commitment deepen? Did behavioral habits increase? The SSIS conflates these, so score changes are theoretically ambiguous. Sports properties using SSIS for evaluation should be aware they are measuring "something related to identification" rather than a theoretically precise construct.
