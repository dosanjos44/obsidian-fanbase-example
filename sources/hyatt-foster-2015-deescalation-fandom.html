Using Identity Work Theory to Understand the De-escalation of Fandom: A Study of Former Fans of National Hockey League Teams

Core Claim

Fandom is not a static identity but an ongoing process requiring continuous "work" to maintain. When fans experience a misalignment between their values and those of the sport object, OR when practical barriers prevent them from engaging in fan practices, they may de-escalate from active fandom to non-fandom. Critically, non-fan identity also requires active maintenance — former fans must continuously enhance the cognitive/emotional conflict and embed practical impediments to sustain their exit.

Key Findings / Arguments

  • Two pathways to de-escalation: (1) Misalignment of fan values (cognitive/emotional conflict between fan's values and sport object's perceived values), OR (2) Barriers to fan practices (practical impediments preventing material enactment of fandom). These can operate independently or simultaneously.

  • Seven empirical themes for exit: sport in general (3/23), hockey specifically (5/23), league-level issues (19/23), team-related (9/23), individual players (5/23), media (6/23), life circumstances (15/23). League-level issues dominated, with the 2004-05 NHL lockout cited by all ten participants who were fans entering that season.

  • Non-fandom requires work: Former fans maintain their non-fan identity by (1) enhancing cognitive/emotional conflict (reinforcing why the sport object's values conflict with theirs) and (2) embedding practical impediments (filling time with activities that preclude fan practices). This is not passive disengagement but active identity work.

  • Three age-based cohorts with distinct de-escalation narratives: younger fans (avg 30) emphasised the lockout, middle-aged (avg 49) were turned off by violence/culture, older (avg 61) cited league expansion destroying the intimacy of the Original Six era.

  • Team success not a standalone factor: Only one participant cited losing as a standalone reason; several cited winning as a reason (attracted bandwagon fans, diluted authentic identity). This contradicts Wann et al. (1996)'s finding that success is a primary cessation factor.

  • Reverse socialisation potential: Several participants speculated their children or grandchildren might pull them back into fandom — a potential mechanism for re-engagement not explored in prior literature.

  • National team support persists: Many former NHL team fans still watch international competition (Olympics, World Juniors), where national identity and perceived authenticity (amateur values, no commercialism) align with their values even when league-level fandom has ended.

Methods

Interpretive qualitative study using semi-structured interviews with 23 former NHL team fans (24 interviewed; one excluded for not meeting criteria). Sample: 20 men, 3 women, aged 18-67 (mean 43), predominantly white, well-educated Ontario residents. Interviews conducted May 2011 to February 2013, averaging 35 minutes. Data collection ended at theoretical saturation. Coding used constant comparison method with iterative codebook development.

Inclusion criteria: Participants had to demonstrate prior attachment/allegiance-stage identification with a former favourite NHL team and currently consider themselves non-fans of any NHL team.

Contributions to Fandom Theory

  1. First empirical study of fandom exit: Directly answers calls from Funk & James (2001) and Mullin et al. (2007) for research on "downward movement" and "dropouts" from the escalator/continuum models.

  2. Identity work theory application: Imports identity work theory from organisational studies to explain fandom as ongoing accomplishment. Being a fan (or non-fan) requires continuous effort — identities don't persist automatically.

  3. De-escalation model: Three-stage model (Maintaining Fandom → Disrupting Fandom → De-escalating) with parallel cognitive/emotional and material/practical tracks. Provides mechanistic account missing from prior descriptive work.

  4. Non-fandom as active identity: Key theoretical insight that non-fandom is not mere absence of fandom but a constructed identity requiring its own maintenance work. This has direct implications for re-engagement strategy.

  5. Multi-level exit causation: Demonstrates that team-fan bonds are nested within sport, hockey, and league contexts; disruption at any level can cascade to team-level de-escalation.

Limitations & Caveats

  • Sample homogeneity: Predominantly white, male, well-educated Ontario residents. Gender and cultural differences in de-escalation may exist but are not captured.

  • Retrospective accounts: Relied on participants' recollections of processes that may have occurred decades earlier. Memory reconstruction may distort actual experience.

  • NHL/hockey specificity: The NHL has unique characteristics (fighting, lockouts, expansion into non-hockey markets) that may not generalise to other sports/leagues. The 2004-05 lockout was an extreme event.

  • No longitudinal tracking: Could not observe de-escalation in real time or verify predictions about future re-engagement.

  • Canadian context: NHL hockey has distinctive cultural significance in Canada; dynamics may differ in US or international contexts.

  • Non-fan sample only: Did not compare with fans who experienced similar disruptions but maintained fandom — cannot isolate what distinguishes those who exit from those who repair.

Applied Note

This paper provides the most actionable framework for understanding lapsed fan recovery:

Which fans are recoverable?

  • Those whose exit was driven by practical impediments (life circumstances, lack of time, relocation) rather than value misalignment are more recoverable. If barriers are removed, fandom can resume.
  • Those whose non-fan identity is weakly maintained (not actively enhancing conflict, not deeply embedding impediments) are more accessible.
  • The finding that children/grandchildren might pull former fans back suggests reverse socialisation as a re-engagement mechanism — targeting families rather than individuals.

Which fans are likely unrecoverable?

  • Those with deep value misalignment (perceive sport/league/team as greedy, violent, inauthentic) require the sport object to change, not just marketing intervention.
  • Those who have embedded strong practical impediments (new sports, new hobbies, new routines) have high switching costs back to fandom.

What must change for re-engagement?
The seven themes tell us what broke — league-level issues (lockouts, expansion, rule changes), team management (greed, disrespect), player behaviour, violence culture. Most of the top causes (19/23 league-level) are not team-controllable. This is sobering: teams may have limited power to recover fans alienated by league-wide factors.

Strategic implication: Maintaining fans is far cheaper than recovering them. Once non-fan identity is established and actively maintained, reversal requires either (1) major changes to the sport object, (2) removal of embedded practical impediments, or (3) competing identity demands (e.g., parenting) that override non-fan identity work. Prevention (keeping fans in the "repairing" stage rather than letting them fully de-escalate) is the key insight for Sporting Cultural Architecture.
