The Career of the Sport Supporter: The Case of the Manchester Storm

Core Claim

Sport fandom should be understood as a career — a fluid, bi-directional trajectory through which individuals move across their life course — rather than as a fixed identity state or categorical type. Drawing on the Chicago School tradition (Hughes, Becker, Goffman, Glaser & Strauss), Crawford argues that supporter identities are processual, socially constructed, and subject to change in both directions. This framing dissolves the problematic authenticity debates that pervade fan typologies and instead focuses attention on the temporal dynamics of how people enter, move through, persist in, regress within, or exit fandom.

Key Findings / Arguments

  • Career trajectory model: Proposes seven stages of supporter career — General Public → Interested → Engaged → Enthusiastic → Devoted → Professional → Apparatus — representing increasing centrality of sport to identity and social networks (p. 227-230)

  • Bi-directional movement: Unlike escalator models, careers can move down as well as up. Supporters regress, lapse, and re-engage throughout the life course. The trajectory is neither linear nor necessarily progressive (p. 230-232)

  • Critique of typologies: Authenticity-based fan typologies (including Giulianotti's) are fundamentally flawed because they treat supporter identities as fixed states rather than fluid positions within an ongoing career. The "real fan" question is unanswerable and unproductive (p. 221-224)

  • Neo-tribal fluidity: Drawing on Maffesoli's neo-tribes concept, Crawford argues contemporary sport support is characterised by fluid, temporary, consumer-oriented attachments rather than fixed community membership. Supporters can hold multiple, overlapping allegiances (p. 224-227)

  • Entry points vary: The Manchester Storm case demonstrates that adult entry into fandom is possible and common — many supporters entered at the Interested or Engaged level as adults through work colleagues, partners, or entertainment seeking, not childhood socialisation (p. 228-229)

  • Apparatus category: The highest career stage — where sport involvement becomes one's occupation (journalists, academics, club employees). These individuals have different structural relationships to sport than other supporters (p. 230)

  • Incorporation/Resistance critique: Rejects the Frankfurt School-derived framework that divides fans into "authentic resisters" and "passive dupes." Both poles are sociologically unhelpful; most supporters occupy complex, shifting positions (p. 222-224)

Methods

Design: Mixed-method case study of Manchester Storm, a British Ice Hockey Superleague team

Data:

  • 41 semi-structured interviews with supporters
  • Survey of 1,400+ supporters
  • 3 years participant observation (1997-2000)

Context: Ice hockey is a "new" sport in the UK, allowing study of adult fan acquisition without the confound of childhood socialisation. Manchester Storm was established 1995, folded 2002.

Strengths: Longitudinal engagement, mixed methods, unique population (adult-acquired fans of non-traditional sport)

Limitations: Single case study, single sport (ice hockey), single nation (UK). The "new sport" context may not generalise to established sports where childhood origination dominates. Sample self-selected through fan networks.

Contributions to Fandom Theory

  1. Temporal dimension: Prior frameworks (Giulianotti, Hunt et al., even PCM) describe positions or stages but treat them statically. Crawford introduces genuine temporality — fandom unfolds across the life course and the same individual moves through multiple positions.

  2. Bi-directionality: The explicit theorisation of downward movement (regression, lapsing, disengagement) is rare in the literature. Most frameworks are implicitly one-directional (fans get more committed or stay the same).

  3. Career as bridge concept: The career framework connects childhood origination (early entry, slow progression) to adult origination (later entry, potentially rapid progression or stagnation) within a single theoretical vocabulary.

  4. Dissolution of authenticity debates: By framing positions as career stages rather than identity types, Crawford sidesteps the unproductive question of who is a "real" fan.

  5. Adult entry routes documented: Empirically demonstrates that adults enter fandom through work colleagues, partners, entertainment seeking, and community participation — not just childhood socialisation spillover.

Limitations & Caveats

  • Single case, unusual context: Manchester Storm was a new team in a non-traditional sport. Findings may not transfer to established teams with multi-generational supporter bases.

  • No longitudinal tracking: Despite the "career" framing, data is cross-sectional. Crawford infers careers from retrospective accounts and cross-sectional variation, not actual tracking of individuals over time.

  • Career stages are analytical, not empirical: The seven stages are proposed conceptually, not derived from empirical clustering or validated through measurement.

  • Neo-tribal framing may overstate fluidity: The Maffesoli-derived emphasis on fluid, temporary allegiances may underweight the genuine fixedness of identity-based fandom documented elsewhere (cf. James 2001 on childhood commitment).

  • UK ice hockey is niche: The supporter base skews middle-class, educated, adult-acquired — not representative of mass-market football fandom.

  • No engagement with PCM: Crawford does not cite Funk & James (2001) or engage with the Psychological Continuum Model, though the frameworks address similar questions from different traditions.

Applied Note

The career framework has significant implications for Sporting Cultural Architecture:

Re-engagement strategy: If fandom is a career that can move bi-directionally, lapsed fans are not "lost" — they have regressed within their career but retain career history. Re-engagement should acknowledge this history rather than treating lapsed fans as new prospects. The Applied Note should focus on understanding where supporters are in their career and what caused regression.

Adult acquisition: The Manchester Storm case demonstrates that adults can enter at various career stages (not just Awareness). Sports properties targeting adult acquisition should identify the entry points (work, partners, entertainment, community) and design pathways from those points.

Against typology segmentation: Crawford's critique suggests that marketing segmentation based on fan "types" is flawed. Supporters are not types; they are careers-in-progress. Segmentation should be based on career position and trajectory direction, not categorical identity.

Lapsed fan interventions: The gap between Enthusiastic and Devoted is critical — this is where careers often stall or regress. Interventions should focus on facilitating the social network integration that characterises higher career stages.

Multiple allegiances: The neo-tribal framing suggests supporters can hold multiple, non-exclusive allegiances. This challenges the assumption that sport fandom is zero-sum competition for loyalty.
