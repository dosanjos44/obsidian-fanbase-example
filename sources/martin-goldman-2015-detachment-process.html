A Process Model of Sport Fan Detachment

Core Claim

Sport fan detachment follows a four-part sequential process: breakdown trigger, iterative decline, disengagement incident, and exit phase. This process model directly addresses the acknowledged gap in the Psychological Continuum Model (Funk & James 2001) regarding downward movement, demonstrating that relationship deterioration unfolds over time through multiple phases rather than occurring as a single event.

Key Findings / Arguments

  • Four-phase sequential model synthesised from customer-brand relationship dissolution literature (Fajer & Schouten 1995; Coulter & Ligas 2000; Halinen & Tähtinen 2002; Tähtinen 2002):

    1. Breakdown trigger — interpersonal, intrapersonal, or structural event that sparks the deterioration process (p. 1032–1033)
    2. Iterative decline — cyclical phase with positive and negative experiences that either reinforce or erode the relationship; not automatic or immediate (p. 1034)
    3. Disengagement incident — determinant event that tips cost/benefit evaluation against continued fandom; can be service, market, self, or other related (p. 1034)
    4. Exit — termination through negotiation or withdrawal; internalisation and sense-making (p. 1034–1035)

  • Iterative decline is the intervention window: "our findings point to the possible effectiveness of restorative actions during the iterative decline leading to the disengagement incident" (p. 1040). Long-term season ticket holders display a longer iterative decline phase.

  • Breakdown triggers documented (p. 1036): stadium experience quality (smoking, drunk/swearing patrons, seating issues), perceived poor value proposition, vehicle safety concerns, lack of response to complaints, dislike of team's playing style, controversial branding decisions ("pink jerseys")

  • Exit modes include: sadness and sense-making, justification of decision, shift to home viewing, occasional single-game ticket purchase (p. 1039)

  • Dual attribution: Some lapsed fans attribute decline to franchise changes (external); others recognise they themselves changed (internal); some perceive both (p. 1039)

Methods

  • Design: Qualitative in-depth interviews with lapsed season ticket holders
  • Sample: N=14 lapsed season ticket holders of a championship-winning Super Rugby franchise in South Africa (saturation reached after 14 interviews)
  • Interview duration: ~40 minutes each
  • Period: Mid-2013
  • Analysis: Directed content analysis using Atlas.ti; deductive coding framework based on proposed process model; pseudonyms used

Contributions to Fandom Theory

  1. Directly addresses PCM's downward movement gap: Funk & James (2001, 2006) acknowledged the need for research on how fans move down the continuum; this paper provides a process account drawn from customer-brand dissolution literature (p. 1031).

  2. Detachment as process, not event: Following Farrelly (2010) and Van der Ven (2007), demonstrates that relationship ending unfolds sequentially through identifiable phases — "process studies are fundamental for gaining an appreciation of dynamic social life" (p. 1031).

  3. Iterative decline as actionable insight: By identifying a cyclical assessment phase between trigger and exit, the paper reveals an intervention window absent from prior work.

  4. Complements Hyatt & Foster (2015): While published the same year and addressing similar phenomena (fandom exit), the approaches differ. Hyatt & Foster focus on why fans exit (value misalignment vs practical barriers) and how identity work enables exit; Martin & Goldman focus on the temporal sequence through which exit unfolds. Together they provide complementary mechanism (Hyatt & Foster) and process (Martin & Goldman) accounts.

Limitations & Caveats

  • Small sample size (N=14): While saturation was reported, the sample is modest. Generalisability to broader populations is limited.

  • Single franchise, single sport: South African Super Rugby context may not translate to other sports, leagues, or cultures. Some triggers (stadium safety, "pink jerseys") are highly context-specific.

  • Retrospective methodology: Participants reconstructed their exit process after the fact; memory reconstruction biases may affect reported sequences.

  • Highly committed starting point: All participants were season ticket holders — the "pinnacle of loyalty" (p. 1031). The process may differ for less-committed fans with shallower attachment.

  • Behavioural focus, limited affective capture: "The study was largely based on behavioral observations, while possible affective changes were not comprehensively captured" (p. 1041).

  • No comparison group: Does not include fans who faced similar triggers but did not exit — cannot distinguish what makes some fans resilient and others vulnerable.

Applied Note

The four-phase model identifies iterative decline as the primary intervention window. Unlike breakdown triggers (often outside franchise control) and disengagement incidents (often too late), the iterative decline phase involves ongoing assessment where the fan relationship can still be saved.

Specific intervention opportunities documented:

  1. Tailored ticket packages: Increase flexibility for fans facing interpersonal or financial constraints — address practical barriers before they compound into exit

  2. Secondary ticket market facilitation: Allow season ticket holders to sell individual game tickets; "teams are able to influence the cost/benefit evaluation" (p. 1040)

  3. Responsive communication: Several participants noted complaints went unanswered. Proactive relationship maintenance and rapid response to negative experiences can interrupt the iterative decline.

  4. Long-term holders get longer runway: "more long-term season ticket holders display a longer iterative decline" (p. 1040) — meaning higher-tenure fans offer more intervention opportunities but also more accumulated grievances.

For Sporting Cultural Architecture: The iterative decline phase corresponds to the "repair" phase in Hyatt & Foster's identity work model. Both papers point to the same strategic conclusion: the window between disruption and exit is where intervention pays off. Once exit is complete and non-fan identity is being actively maintained, recovery becomes far more difficult. Monitor for early warning signs during the iterative phase — attendance decline, merchandise lapse, complaint volume — and intervene before the disengagement incident crystallises the decision.
