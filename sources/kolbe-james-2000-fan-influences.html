An Identification and Examination of Influences That Shape the Creation of a Professional Team Fan

Core Claim

Becoming a sports team fan is a socialisation process in which different agents exert influence at different life stages: fathers dominate preteen fan creation (57.8%), while players/coaches become primary influence agents for those who become fans as adults (31.9%). Most fans become "true fans" between ages 6–15 (modal age 10), and the city-team linkage serves as a persistent loyalty anchor independent of interpersonal agents.

Key Findings / Arguments

  • Father dominance in childhood: 38.8% of respondents reported "father" as the single most influential person in becoming a fan — the highest category by far (p. 19, Table 1).

  • Age-differentiated influence agents (p. 22, Table 5):
    | Life Stage | Father | Family | Players/Coaches | Friends |
    |------------|--------|--------|-----------------|---------|
    | Preteen (≤12) | 57.8% | 12.8% | 20.6% | 1.4% |
    | Adolescent (13–20) | 40.0% | 12.3% | 28.5% | 8.5% |
    | Adult (21+) | 10.1% | 26.1% | 31.9% | 6.7% |

  • Cognitive threshold at ~age 7: 56% of respondents became "true fans" before age 15; modal age was 10 years old (16.7%). Almost no one became a true fan before age 6 (p. 22, Table 4).

  • City-team linkage as influence factor: Non-interpersonal factors rated highest: "growing up in Cleveland" (M=6.19), "hometown team" (M=6.17), "Cleveland institution" (M=5.99) on 7-point scale (p. 20, Table 2).

  • Game-day atmosphere matters: Stadium experience (M=6.05) and attending first game (M=5.06) rated highly; tailgating and training camp visits were less influential (p. 20).

  • Community of fans valued: Respondents valued knowing Browns fans exist worldwide (M=5.78), liked being part of a "community of fans" (M=5.64), and felt a connective bond with other fans (M=5.35) — yet did not require Cleveland origin for community membership (p. 21).

  • Self-originated fans: 7.8% said they themselves (not another person) were the reason they became fans (p. 19).

Methods

  • Design: Mail survey to Cleveland Browns PSL/season ticket holders (N=518, 25.9% response rate)
  • Sample: Long-term fans (average 30+ years as fan, 21 years as season ticket holder); 86.7% male; modal age 41–45; 79.3% married
  • Stratified sampling: Four strata by ticket-holder tenure and Dawg Pound membership
  • Measures: Free-response for most influential person; 7-point Likert scales for influence factors; self-reported age of becoming "true fan"
  • Analysis: Frequencies, means, chi-square for age-group differences

Contributions to Fandom Theory

  1. First empirical mapping of age-differentiated socialising agents: Demonstrates that fathers dominate childhood but become less influential as the age of fan entry increases, while players/coaches become more influential for adolescents and adults.

  2. Confirms cognitive developmental threshold: Corroborates James (1997/2001) finding that "true" fandom typically forms after age 6–7, when sufficient cognitive maturity exists for enduring commitment.

  3. Introduces place attachment as socialisation dimension: City-team linkage ("hometown team") emerged as the strongest non-interpersonal influence — distinct from interpersonal agents and suggesting topophilic attachment is foundational, not secondary.

  4. Documents fan community as valued feature: Fans value belonging to a dispersed community of fellow fans, even while not requiring geographic origin for membership — an early hint of what later literature would call brand community.

  5. Provides entry-end complement to exit research: Together with Hyatt & Foster (2015) on de-escalation, offers a complete lifespan view: Kolbe & James documents how people enter fandom careers; Hyatt & Foster documents how they exit.

Limitations & Caveats

  • Single team, single sport: Cleveland Browns (NFL) only. May not generalise to other sports, other leagues, or non-US contexts.

  • Retrospective self-report: Adults recalling when they became "true fans" — memory distortion possible, especially for childhood-origin fans.

  • Highly committed sample: PSL holders who paid during a 3-year team hiatus are extreme loyalists. Findings may not represent casual fans or lapsed fans.

  • 86.7% male sample: Cannot address female fan socialisation or gender differences in influence agents.

  • No definition of "true fan": Respondents were not given criteria for what constitutes being a "true" fan — introduces subjective variation.

  • Pre-social media era (2000): The digital/online fan community dynamics that dominate contemporary fandom did not exist.

  • Does not test mechanism: Documents which agents influence but not how the influence operates (frequency, emotional quality, transmission process).

Applied Note

This paper has direct implications for childhood fan development strategy and the design of "Family Sections" or youth-centric marketing:

Father-child bonding = long-term ROI: The finding that fathers are the dominant socialising agent for childhood fans (57.8% of preteen fans) suggests that investments in father-child shared experiences at team events have compound returns. Properties should design pricing, scheduling, amenities, and programming specifically for father-child dyads — not generic "family" offerings. The loyalty infrastructure created through father-child bonding at ages 6–10 produces fans who remain for 30+ years (as the Browns sample demonstrates).

City-team linkage as developmental asset: The extremely high ratings for "hometown team" and "growing up in Cleveland" suggest that place attachment is not merely contextual but foundational to loyalty. Properties should actively cultivate the city-team linkage through civic identity messaging, local integration, and community visibility — particularly for young fans whose place identity is still forming.

Stage-appropriate touchpoints: Different agents matter at different ages. For preteens, create father-child touchpoints. For adolescents and adults, player/coach visibility and accessibility become more important — meet-and-greets, player-driven content, and coach accessibility have higher ROI for later-stage acquisition than for childhood cultivation.

Community membership as belonging signal: Fans valued belonging to a dispersed community of fellow fans without requiring geographic origin for membership. This suggests fan community platforms (now digital) serve a belonging function beyond information exchange — they signal that membership is available and meaningful. Properties should make community membership visible and accessible to prospective fans.
