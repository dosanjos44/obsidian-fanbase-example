Sport and social media research: A review

Core Claim

Social media have fundamentally shifted sport marketing from a broadcast (goods-dominant) paradigm to a relational (service-dominant) paradigm, where value is co-created through ongoing interaction between brands and fans rather than delivered via one-way communication. This shift positions engagement — not reach — as the central metric for evaluating social media effectiveness.

Key Findings / Arguments

  • S-D Logic as organising framework: The transition from goods-dominant to service-dominant logic (Vargo & Lusch 2004) provides the theoretical backbone for understanding social media in sport. Value is not embedded in the product/content; value is created through the consumption process itself (p. 2).

  • Value co-creation is central: In contrast to traditional marketing where organisations deliver value propositions for passive consumption, social media enables fans and brands to create meaning together through interaction, sharing, and user-generated content (p. 2).

  • Relationship marketing operationalises S-D logic: Social media provides the operational mechanism for relationship marketing — "the efforts of brands to build and maintain bonds with customers and other stakeholders through mutual exchange and interactivity" (Grönroos 2004, cited p. 2).

  • Engagement drives relationship formation: Psychological engagement (cognitive, affective, behavioural) is "the engine room driving the formation of meaningful relationships" (p. 3). Engagement precedes and enables co-creation.

  • Three-category taxonomy of social media research: (1) Strategic — role and function of social media from brand perspective; (2) Operational — day-to-day utilisation and content; (3) User-focused — fan motivations, profiles, preferences (pp. 3–4).

  • 52% of 70 reviewed articles lacked explicit theory: Sport social media research is largely atheoretical and descriptive; over half did not state a theoretical framework (p. 10).

  • PCM as relevant framework: Filo et al. recommend the Psychological Continuum Model (Funk & James 2006) for understanding how social media engagement varies by fan stage and how social media can facilitate stage movement (p. 12).

  • User-generated content appeals to low-identifiers: Kwak et al. (2010) found that less-identified consumers view user-generated content more favourably, suggesting co-creation plays a role in engaging newcomers (p. 9).

Methods

Systematic literature review of 70 journal articles published in English-language sport management journals through 2014. Selection via informal channels (research team, "invisible college"), primary channels (manual search of 8 journals), and secondary channels (Google Scholar, ancestry approach). Articles categorised by research objectives and methods.

Scope: Defined social media as "new media technologies facilitating interactivity and co-creation that allow for the development and sharing of user-generated content among and between organisations and individuals" (p. 2). Excluded fantasy sport, eCommerce, and broadcast extensions where interactivity was not core.

Contributions to Fandom Theory

  1. First systematic application of S-D logic to sport social media: Provides a coherent theoretical frame that had been absent from the field's scattered empirical work.

  2. Connects engagement to relationship marketing: Positions engagement as the mechanism linking social media practice to relationship outcomes — not as an end in itself but as the driver of deeper connection.

  3. Identifies co-creation as the distinctive feature of social media: Differentiates social media from broadcast media not by platform but by the active role of consumers in producing meaning.

  4. Calls for PCM integration: Explicitly recommends testing how social media engagement varies by PCM stage and whether social media can facilitate the critical attraction→attachment transition.

  5. Documents the state of the field: Reveals that sport social media research is predominantly descriptive, North American, and cross-sectional — identifying clear directions for theoretical and methodological development.

Limitations & Caveats

  • Review, not primary research: No new data; findings are synthesis of prior work with its inherited limitations.

  • Limited to sport management journals: Excludes tourism, events, leisure, and mainstream business literature that may address similar phenomena.

  • English-language only: Non-English scholarship excluded.

  • Pre-2015 literature: The social media landscape has evolved substantially since the review period (pre-Instagram dominance, pre-TikTok, pre-algorithmic feed prioritisation).

  • S-D logic application is retrospective: The reviewed studies were not designed with S-D logic in mind; Filo et al. impose this frame interpretively.

  • No empirical test of co-creation's impact on fan development: The review identifies the theoretical potential but does not provide evidence that co-creation actually produces attachment or allegiance.

Applied Note

Filo et al. provides the theoretical vocabulary for moving beyond vanity metrics in sport social media strategy. The field's obsession with reach, likes, and follower counts reflects a goods-dominant mentality: content as product, audience as recipients. S-D logic reorients measurement toward co-creation and engagement — not because engagement sounds better, but because value is created in the interaction, not delivered to passive consumers.

Beyond Vanity Metrics: The Co-Creation Imperative

For federations and agencies, this means:

  1. Measure engagement quality, not quantity: A fan who creates content, participates in discussion, or contributes to community meaning is co-creating — and building attachment. A fan who scrolls past content has received a value proposition but may not have created value.

  2. Design for participation: Social media strategy should invite contribution, not just consumption. User-generated content, fan polls, comment engagement, and community challenges are co-creation mechanisms. Broadcast-style posting is goods-dominant even on a relational platform.

  3. Social media as socialisation agent: The review suggests social media can function as a modern socialising agent — enabling the "active immersion" that Delia & Katz (2019) identified as necessary for adult conversion. A fan who participates in online community may be undergoing the same identity development that travel-immersed fans experience.

  4. Stage-appropriate social media tactics: Following Filo et al.'s recommendation to integrate PCM: awareness-stage fans need exposure; attraction-stage fans need hedonic content; but moving fans to attachment requires social bonding and identity-linking — which co-creation can provide. The question is whether a social media interaction builds personal meaning or just delivers entertainment.

  5. The structural question remains: S-D logic explains that co-creation produces value; it does not address what kind of relationship is being formed. Co-creation could produce consumer-type fans (Giulianotti's "fans") or traditional-type supporters — the platform enables both. The content and context of co-creation determine the outcome.
